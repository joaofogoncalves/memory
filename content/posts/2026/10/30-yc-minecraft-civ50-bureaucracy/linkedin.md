One AI agent tossed a cooked chicken to another, a third agent picked it up and ate it, and the colony held a three-minute no-blame inquiry that ended with a written protocol: rations go in named chests and are never thrown.

That comes from a Y Combinator Labs experiment with Olam Labs, where 50 agents shared a survival Minecraft world for three hours with one goal, build a thriving civilization. The colony did not thrive. The agents sent about 5,900 messages, 22 of the 34 agent deaths happened on the first night, and they spent most of the run in crowded shelters rationing food.

What they built instead was paperwork. Nobody held a vote, but 46 of the other 49 agents sent their first message to Ada, the first name in the directory, and she became the leader with 616 DMs. Four agents spent 11 minutes appointing a sole fertilizer operator for one piece of bone meal on one wheat block, and agents asked permission to remove grass. A ration ledger, permits and appointed operators followed.

I find this funny, and also useful. The hard part of a group of agents was never moving or crafting. It was deciding who does what, who may touch what, and how to confirm that something actually happened, and the agents invented about the same answers a new team does: a ledger, an owner for every task, and a rule written down after the first mess. YC's authors say it resembles what they see with coding agents, which also get overly cautious. I would rather learn that in a Minecraft sandbox than in a production system.
