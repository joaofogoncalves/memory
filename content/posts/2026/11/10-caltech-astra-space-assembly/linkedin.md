A robot from Caltech assembled the third bay of a 1.4-metre-diameter antenna in orbit, after the payload launched with the first two of 11 bays already built.

That is the real scale of the ASTRA demonstration that Caltech and DARPA announced on 5 November. The posts making the rounds describe a robot building a structure in space on its own. A new bay did go together in orbit, without astronauts. The two prebuilt bays were pushed out first, and the team ran into time limitations and only finished that initial section. The mission ends in December, and Caltech says it appears unlikely the remaining eight bays will be completed.

Andrew Detor, who runs DARPA's NOM4D program, compares the approach to Tinkertoys or K'nex: individual members join at nodes, one section at a time. He says there are no complicated robotics or advanced algorithms, and that this is how the work scales. The program's aim is structures hundreds of metres or kilometres across, the kind you cannot fold into a rocket fairing.

A third bay is a small structure. The useful result is that a simple repeating joint worked in space. The next test is whether those same joints can keep adding bays until the antenna is actually complete.
